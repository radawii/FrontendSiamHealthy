Overview

Magnesium oxide, or MgO, is an inorganic compound formed from the elements magnesium and oxygen. It is a white powder, and elemental magnesium makes up approximately 60% of the weight of the pure compound. Because of this property, it is one of the forms of magnesium most commonly used as a raw material in dietary supplements.

Magnesium is involved in the activity of more than 300 enzyme systems in the body, ranging from protein synthesis and the production and use of energy to signalling between nerve cells and the regulation of muscle function. In addition, approximately 50–60% of the body's total magnesium is found in bone, with most of the remainder in muscle and soft tissue, reflecting the importance of this mineral for both the structure and the function of the body.

Key functions

  • Magnesium contributes to normal functioning of the nervous system and muscles
  • Magnesium contributes to normal energy-yielding metabolism
  • Magnesium contributes to electrolyte balance
  • Magnesium contributes to the maintenance of normal bones
  • Magnesium contributes to the maintenance of normal teeth
  • Magnesium contributes to normal protein synthesis

How it works in the body

Magnesium and its mechanisms in the body

1. Muscle contraction and relaxation

Every movement of the body, from walking and exercising to the beating of the heart, involves several minerals working together. Magnesium works alongside calcium and potassium in regulating the movement of ions across cell membranes, which is an essential part of the process of muscle contraction and relaxation.

2. The nervous system and electrical signalling

Nerve cells use electrical signals and neurotransmitters to transmit information between the brain and different parts of the body. Magnesium is involved in regulating ion channels and the activity of certain neural receptors, which are fundamental mechanisms of communication within the nervous system.

3. Energy production and use at the cellular level

Most of the energy that cells use for their various activities is in the form of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). ATP is usually bound to a magnesium ion in the form of Mg-ATP so that enzymes can use its energy in biochemical reactions. Magnesium is therefore a key component of energy use at the cellular level.

4. Bone structure and mineral balance

In addition to calcium and phosphorus, magnesium is a mineral found in bone structure and is involved in regulating calcium balance, as well as in biochemical processes related to vitamin D. This reflects the fact that the maintenance of normal bones does not depend on any single nutrient alone.

Food sources of magnesium

• Nuts and seeds, such as pumpkin seeds, almonds and cashews

• Green leafy vegetables, such as spinach, kale and chard

• Whole grains, such as brown rice, oats and wholewheat bread

• Legumes, such as black beans, red kidney beans, soybeans and chickpeas

• Other foods, such as dark chocolate, avocado, milk and yoghurt

• Dietary supplements, both as a single mineral and in formulas combined with other vitamins or minerals

Scientific evidence

1. Magnesium oxide and absorption

A 2019 study examined magnesium absorption in 14 healthy male volunteers, comparing magnesium oxide with magnesium citrate using blood and urine magnesium levels as indicators after ingestion.

The study found that magnesium citrate was better absorbed than magnesium oxide, indicating that chemical form is one of the factors affecting mineral absorption. However, this study had a relatively small number of participants and assessed outcomes only over the short term.

2. Magnesium, muscle function and cramps

A 2020 Cochrane systematic review pooled 11 trials with a total of 735 participants to evaluate the effect of magnesium supplementation on muscle cramps in several populations.

The review found that magnesium supplementation is unlikely to provide a clinically meaningful benefit for preventing cramps in older adults in general, while the evidence in pregnant women remains inconsistent. Although magnesium is essential for muscle function, additional magnesium intake does not mean it will help reduce cramps in every case.

3. Magnesium and bone mineral density

Information from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) states that magnesium is involved in bone formation and in regulating hormones that affect calcium balance, and several observational studies have found an association between dietary magnesium intake and bone mineral density.

However, this association cannot yet confirm that supplementation with magnesium oxide specifically will help increase bone mineral density or reduce the risk of fractures, so further clinical studies are still needed.

*This information is provided to educate about magnesium oxide and is not advice for diagnosing, preventing or treating any disease.

Safety information

Taking high amounts of magnesium may cause loose stools, nausea or stomach discomfort.

People with impaired kidney function should consult a doctor before taking dietary supplements containing magnesium, as the kidneys play a role in excreting excess magnesium from the body.

Magnesium may interfere with the absorption of certain antibiotics, such as tetracyclines and quinolones, as well as bisphosphonates, so consult a doctor or pharmacist about appropriate timing of intake.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
