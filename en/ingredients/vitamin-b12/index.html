Overview

Vitamin B12, or cobalamin, is a water-soluble vitamin that contains the mineral cobalt. It is found naturally in animal foods such as meat, fish, eggs and dairy products. Unlike many other B vitamins, vitamin B12 can be stored in the liver for long periods, so it may take several years before a deficiency produces clear symptoms.

The key functions of vitamin B12 are its involvement in red blood cell formation and DNA synthesis, which is necessary for cell division. It is also involved in forming and maintaining the myelin sheath that surrounds nerve fibres, helping the nervous system transmit signals normally, and plays a role in nutrient metabolism so that the body can make use of nutrients.

However, getting vitamin B12 from food does not mean the body will absorb it fully, because this vitamin relies on stomach acid and a protein called intrinsic factor to be absorbed in the small intestine. Older adults, people who eat mainly plant-based diets and people with nutrient absorption problems may therefore be at risk of insufficient vitamin B12 intake, even if some of them eat normally.

Key functions

  • Vitamin B12 helps produce substances needed for red blood cell formation
  • Vitamin B12 contributes to the normal function of the nervous system and brain
  • Vitamin B12 contributes to normal energy-yielding metabolism
  • Vitamin B12 contributes to the normal function of the immune system

How it works in the body

Vitamin B12 and its mechanisms in the body

1. The nervous system and nerve signalling

Vitamin B12 is important for forming and protecting the myelin sheath, a fatty layer that surrounds nerve fibres and helps nerve signals travel efficiently. Having enough vitamin B12 is therefore part of maintaining normal nervous system function.

2. The blood system and cell formation

Vitamin B12 works together with folate in DNA synthesis, which is necessary for cell division and development, especially of red blood cell precursors in the bone marrow. If the body lacks vitamin B12, red blood cell formation may become abnormal.

3. Metabolism and energy use

Vitamin B12 acts as a coenzyme in the metabolism of certain nutrients, helping the body convert substrates for use in cellular energy production.

Unique properties

Food sources of vitamin B12

• Meat and organ meats, such as beef, beef liver and chicken liver

• Fish and seafood, such as salmon, tuna, shellfish and certain other seafood

• Eggs and dairy products, such as chicken eggs, milk, yoghurt and cheese

• Foods fortified with vitamin B12, such as some cereals, some plant-based milk formulations and nutritional yeast with added vitamin B12

• Dietary supplements, such as B-complex vitamins, multivitamins and dedicated vitamin B12 products

Scientific evidence

1. Vitamin B12 and nervous system function and memory

A 2021 systematic review pooled 16 randomised trials with a total of 6,276 participants to assess the effects of supplementing vitamin B12 alone or together with other B vitamins. It found no clear evidence that such supplementation improves thinking and memory function in people without a clear vitamin B12 deficiency or severe neurological disorders.

2. Vitamin B12 and red blood cell formation

Clinical information from the NIH explains that vitamin B12 is necessary for DNA synthesis and normal red blood cell formation. Vitamin B12 deficiency may cause abnormal red blood cell formation, including megaloblastic anaemia, which reflects the importance of this vitamin to the blood system.

3. Vitamin B12, fatigue and energy metabolism

Although vitamin B12 plays a role in energy metabolism, clinical studies have not yet found sufficient evidence that vitamin B12 supplementation reduces fatigue in people who are not deficient. A 2021 analysis found only one eligible study on fatigue, so no conclusion can yet be drawn about its effectiveness.

This information is provided for education about vitamin B12. It is not advice for diagnosing, preventing or treating disease.

Safety information

• People who eat mainly vegetarian or vegan diets, older adults and people with nutrient absorption problems may be at risk of insufficient vitamin B12 intake.

• Certain drugs, such as metformin and antacids in the proton pump inhibitor group, may affect the absorption or levels of vitamin B12 when used for a long time.

• People who experience numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, unusual fatigue, or suspect a vitamin B12 deficiency should consult a doctor to assess the cause. Vitamins should not be taken as a substitute for diagnostic testing.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
