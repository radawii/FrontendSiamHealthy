Overview

Ginger is a kitchen herb that many people know for its aroma and hot, spicy taste, but behind this distinctive flavour lies something that has caught scientists' attention, particularly gingerols and shogaols, key compounds that have been studied in relation to the digestive system and the body's functions.

Ginger has the scientific name Zingiber officinale Roscoe and belongs to the Zingiberaceae family. The part most commonly used is the underground rhizome, which can be consumed fresh, dried or processed, and each form may have a different composition of key compounds.

Interestingly, fresh ginger usually contains 6-gingerol as a key component, while drying or heating can convert some gingerols into shogaols. This difference is one reason why researchers are interested in the different forms of ginger, particularly in studying their biological properties and their association with the function of the digestive tract.

Key functions

  • Contains key compounds known as gingerols and shogaols, which have antioxidant properties
  • Is associated with gastric motility and the functioning of the digestive system
  • Contains key compounds that have been studied in relation to the response to inflammatory processes at the cellular level
  • Contains essential oil components that give it its characteristic aroma and compounds that give it its hot, spicy taste

How it works in the body

Ginger and how it works in the body

1. Gastric motility

Some compounds in ginger have been studied in relation to the function of smooth muscle in the digestive tract, including the process of moving food from the stomach to the small intestine, known as gastric emptying.

2. Digestive tract responses

Gingerols and shogaols have been studied in relation to signalling through certain receptors in the digestive tract and nervous system, including pathways involving the neurotransmitter serotonin.

3. Cellular response processes

Laboratory studies have found that gingerols and shogaols can interact with certain cellular signalling pathways involved in oxidative stress and the inflammatory response.

Unique properties

Forms and consumption of ginger

Ginger is an herb that can be consumed in many forms, from a fresh cooking ingredient to a wide range of processed health extracts.

• Fresh ginger: the fresh rhizome is sliced, shredded or juiced for use as a spice in cooking and in drinks, providing a hot, spicy taste and the aroma of its natural essential oils.

• Ginger tea and instant ginger powder: dried ginger is broken into pieces for brewing as tea, or made into a ready-to-mix drink powder, suitable for drinking to relax and for general health care.

• Pickled ginger: young ginger is pickled in vinegar and sugar and is commonly eaten as a side dish in Asian cuisine to cleanse the palate and complement the flavour of food.

• Ginger extract: ginger goes through an extraction process to standardise the content of key compounds, such as gingerols and shogaols, to consistent proportions. It is commonly filled into dietary supplements in capsule or tablet form for convenient intake.

Scientific evidence

1. Studies on gastric motility

Reviews of clinical evidence on ginger have found that some studies report effects on the time it takes for food to leave the stomach, but the results are not consistent across all trials, and there are limitations in the number of participants, product forms and methods of measurement. It therefore cannot yet be clearly concluded that all types of ginger help the digestive system work better.

2. Clinical evidence on nausea

Several systematic reviews have found that some forms of ginger may help reduce the severity of nausea in pregnant women. However, the quality of the evidence varies, and study results on nausea from travel, surgery or chemotherapy are not entirely consistent, so these results should not be used to claim the effectiveness of every ginger extract formula.

3. Studies on the inflammatory response

Reviews pooling clinical studies have found changes in certain markers related to oxidative stress and inflammation, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and malondialdehyde (MDA). However, the level of certainty of the evidence varies and is not yet sufficient to draw conclusions about preventing or treating disease.

This information is provided for educational purposes about ginger and is not advice for diagnosing, preventing or treating any disease.

Safety information

• Taking ginger or ginger extract may cause heartburn, stomach discomfort, diarrhoea or irritation of the mouth and throat in some people.

• People taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet medicines should consult a doctor before using ginger extract, as there are concerns about herb-drug interactions.

• People with gallstones or bile duct disease should consult a doctor before using ginger extract, especially high-concentration products.

• Pregnant and breastfeeding women should consult a healthcare professional before using ginger in supplement form, even though the use of ginger in pregnant women has been studied.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
