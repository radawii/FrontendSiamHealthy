Overview

Black pepper may seem like just a spice that many people use to add flavour to food, but in scientific circles the active compounds hidden in peppercorns have attracted more interest than their heat, especially piperine, which has been studied for its ability to alter the body's absorption and handling of certain substances.

Black pepper has the scientific name Piper nigrum L. and belongs to the family Piperaceae. The black pepper used as a spice comes from fruit harvested before it is fully ripe and then dried, which gives the skin its dark, wrinkled appearance. This differs from white pepper, which undergoes a process to remove the outer skin layer.

The highlight of black pepper is piperine, an alkaloid that contributes to its pungency and has been studied as a substance that may affect the bioavailability of certain compounds. A widely noted example is the study of piperine used together with curcumin from turmeric.

Key functions

  • Contains piperine, a key alkaloid that gives its characteristic pungent heat
  • Has been studied in relation to altering the bioavailability of certain substances
  • Is associated with the absorption and metabolism of substances in the body
  • Contains antioxidants in laboratory studies

How it works in the body

Black pepper and its mechanisms in the body

1. Absorption of bioactive compounds

Piperine has been studied in relation to changes in the amount of certain substances that enter the bloodstream after ingestion. It may affect absorption, transport of substances and their metabolism in the digestive tract or related organs.

2. Function of enzymes and transport proteins

Laboratory studies have found that piperine can interact with certain enzymes and transport proteins involved in the body's handling of substances, such as the CYP enzyme system and the transport protein P-glycoprotein.

3. Perception of pungency

Piperine is associated with the activation of certain sensory receptors, such as TRPV1, which is involved in perceiving heat and irritation. The pungency of black pepper therefore has a different chemical basis from capsaicin found in chilli peppers, even though they share some of the same sensory receptors.

Unique properties

Forms and consumption of black pepper

Black pepper is a spice used widely around the world, and it is processed for consumption in forms ranging from household use to the dietary supplement industry, as follows:

• Whole and ground black peppercorns: dried whole peppercorns, or peppercorns ground finely or coarsely, are used as a main cooking spice, providing pungent heat and a characteristic aroma from natural essential oils.

• Black pepper extract: black peppercorns undergo an extraction process to control the content of the active compound piperine at a standardised, consistent concentration. It is commonly filled into dietary supplements in capsule form, or used together with other nutrients to support the body's absorption.

• Black pepper oleoresin: extracted with solvents to draw out both the pungency and the concentrated aroma in the form of a viscous liquid. It is commonly used in the food-processing industry to flavour products to a consistent standard.

Scientific evidence

1. Studies on piperine used with curcumin in humans

A study by Shoba et al. examined curcumin used together with piperine in healthy volunteers and found that giving 20 milligrams of piperine together with 2 grams of curcumin greatly increased the bioavailability of curcumin compared with curcumin alone.

However, this study had a small number of participants, used specific amounts of the substances and measured blood levels of curcumin. It did not prove that increased absorption leads to greater health benefits, or that the same results apply to every product formulation.

2. Evidence on the absorption of other substances

A study by Badmaev et al. (1999) examined piperine used together with beta-carotene in humans and found differences in blood beta-carotene levels between the group that also received piperine and the comparison group.

The results support the possibility that piperine may alter the bioavailability of certain substances, but they are not sufficient to conclude that piperine increases the absorption of all vitamins, minerals or nutrients.

3. Evidence on the digestive system and overall health

Although black pepper has a long history of use as a spice, clinical research in humans directly assessing the effects of black pepper extract on digestive function is still limited.

Much of the data on antioxidant activity and inflammatory processes comes from cell or animal studies, so it should not be used to confirm that black pepper can prevent or treat gastrointestinal disorders in humans.

This information is provided for education about black pepper. It is not advice for diagnosing, preventing or treating disease.

Safety information

• Consuming large amounts of black pepper or its extract may cause gastrointestinal irritation or stomach discomfort in some people.

• Piperine may alter the absorption and elimination of certain drugs, so consult a doctor or pharmacist before using products containing concentrated piperine together with regular medications.

• People with underlying conditions or who take drugs whose levels must be closely controlled should take particular care.

• Pregnant and breastfeeding women should avoid using concentrated piperine extracts without advice from a healthcare professional, as safety data are still limited.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
