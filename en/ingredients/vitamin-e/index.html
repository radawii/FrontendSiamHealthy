Vitamin EAn Essential Nutrient That Protects Cells from Within

Vitamin E is a fat-soluble vitamin that plays an important role in protecting cells from oxidative stress and is also involved in the normal function of the immune system. Vitamin E is found in vegetable oils, nuts, seeds and certain vegetables.

Overview

When people talk about vitamin E, many think of skincare products or antioxidants, but the key role of this vitamin takes place at a level far smaller than the skin: the cell membrane, the structure that encloses each cell and maintains the environment inside the body's cells.

Vitamin E is a group of fat-soluble nutrients comprising 8 natural forms, divided into 4 tocopherols and 4 tocotrienols. Alpha-tocopherol is the form that the human body mainly maintains and uses to meet its vitamin E requirements.

What makes vitamin E special is its ability to act as an antioxidant in lipid-rich areas, especially cell membranes, which contain fatty acids that may be affected by oxidation. Vitamin E therefore contributes to protecting these lipids, supporting normal cellular function, immune function and communication between cells.

Key functions

  • Vitamin E contributes to the protection of cells from free radicals
  • Vitamin E contributes to antioxidant processes
  • Involved in the normal function of the immune system
  • Involved in cell signalling and cellular function

How it works in the body

Vitamin E and its mechanisms in the body

1. Protecting the lipids in cell membranes

Cell membranes are made up of several types of lipids, especially unsaturated fatty acids that are sensitive to oxidation. Vitamin E can act as an antioxidant by helping to stop the chain reactions that may damage these lipids, and so contributes to maintaining the integrity of cell membranes.

2. Working together with other antioxidants

When vitamin E acts against oxidation, its chemical structure changes. Vitamin C and other antioxidant systems in the body can take part in converting vitamin E back to its active form. This mechanism reflects the fact that many antioxidants work together rather than in complete isolation.

3. Involvement in immune function

Vitamin E is involved in the function of immune cells, including maintaining the condition of cell membranes and signalling between cells. Adequate vitamin E intake is therefore important for the normal function of the immune system.

Scientific evidence

1. Studies on protecting cells from oxidative stress

Vitamin E is recognised as a fat-soluble antioxidant, particularly alpha-tocopherol, which helps protect the fatty acids in cell membranes from oxidation.

Information from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) states that vitamin E is an essential nutrient that plays a role in immune function and various processes within cells. However, high intakes of vitamin E from dietary supplements have not consistently shown additional health benefits in clinical trials.

2. Studies on skin health

Vitamin E is a component of the skin's antioxidant system, and its role in protecting the lipids in skin cells from oxidation, as well as its interaction with vitamin C, has been studied.

However, clinical evidence on taking vitamin E orally to improve the appearance of the skin, reduce wrinkles or slow skin ageing is not yet sufficient to clearly confirm its effectiveness, especially in people who already get enough vitamin E from food.

3. Studies on the immune system

A trial by Meydani et al. (1997) in healthy older adults found that vitamin E supplementation at a defined dose was associated with changes in certain markers of the immune response.

However, this result does not mean that vitamin E supplementation will help reduce the likelihood of infection in all populations, as other trials have produced different results, and changes in immune markers do not always equal clinical benefit.

4. Studies on long-term health

The SELECT trial, which studied more than 35,000 men, found that vitamin E supplementation at 400 IU per day, in the form used in the trial, was associated with an increased risk of prostate cancer compared with placebo during follow-up.

This finding shows that high intakes of vitamin E from dietary supplements are not always beneficial and may carry risks different from normal vitamin E intake through food.

This information is provided to educate about vitamin E and is not advice for diagnosing, preventing or treating any disease.

Safety information

• Taking high doses of supplemental vitamin E may increase the risk of bleeding, especially when used together with anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines.

• People with fat malabsorption disorders may be at risk of vitamin E deficiency and should be assessed by a healthcare professional.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
