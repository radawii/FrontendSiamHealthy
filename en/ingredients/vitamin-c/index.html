Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid)More Than Just a Nutrient Associated with the Common Cold

Vitamin C is a water-soluble vitamin and an essential nutrient that the human body cannot produce on its own. It plays a role in collagen formation, the normal function of the immune system, and increasing the absorption of iron from plant-based foods. It is found in a wide variety of vegetables and fruits.

Overview

When people think of vitamin C, many picture looking after their health during a cold, but vitamin C plays a far more important role in the body than that. It is involved in collagen formation, immune system function and the absorption of iron from food, all of which are processes related to the normal functioning of the body.

Because the human body cannot synthesise vitamin C itself, it must be obtained regularly from food. Common food sources include guava, oranges, kiwi, bell peppers, papaya and broccoli. Getting enough vitamin C from a varied diet is therefore an important foundation of everyday health care.

Key functions

  • Vitamin C contributes to antioxidant processes
  • Vitamin C contributes to collagen formation for the normal function of the skin
  • Vitamin C contributes to the normal function of the immune system
  • Vitamin C contributes to the protection of cells from free radicals
  • Vitamin C increases iron absorption

How it works in the body

What does vitamin C do?

1. Collagen formation

Vitamin C is required for the function of enzymes involved in collagen formation. Collagen is a key structural protein of the skin, blood vessels, cartilage and connective tissue, so getting enough vitamin C is important for maintaining the normal function of these tissues.

2. Immune system function

Vitamin C plays a role in the function of many types of immune cells and is involved in the body's natural response processes. However, getting enough vitamin C does not mean that taking vitamin C supplements will definitely prevent colds.

3. Protecting cells from oxidative stress

Vitamin C is a water-soluble antioxidant. It plays a role in controlling oxidation reactions that occur in the body and is involved in maintaining cellular balance.

4. Iron absorption

Vitamin C helps increase the absorption of non-heme iron, which is found in plant foods such as beans, grains and leafy green vegetables. Eating foods containing vitamin C together with these foods is therefore one way to help the body absorb iron from a meal more effectively.

Vitamin C and its mechanisms in the body

Vitamin C's main mechanism is acting as an electron donor in biochemical reactions at the cellular level. This property allows vitamin C to contribute to the antioxidant processes that counter free radicals generated naturally by energy metabolism, and helps reduce damage to cell structures caused by oxidative stress.

At the tissue-structure level, vitamin C acts as an essential cofactor for enzymes in the collagen-formation process. Collagen is the main structural protein that helps the skin, blood vessels, cartilage and gums maintain their normal function. Vitamin C also contributes to the normal function of the immune system and helps the body absorb iron from food more effectively.

Because the body cannot store excess vitamin C over the long term, the digestive tract and kidneys regulate vitamin C levels to keep them in balance. Absorption is reduced when high amounts are taken in, and any excess the body does not use is naturally excreted in the urine. This mechanism helps keep the body's vitamin C levels within a range suitable for the normal functioning of its various systems.

Unique properties

When should you take vitamin C?

For people taking vitamin C as a dietary supplement, it can generally be taken at various times of the day. There is no need to take it only in the morning or before bed, as there is not yet clear evidence that any particular time consistently provides greater benefit.

If vitamin C causes stomach discomfort, taking it with or after a meal may make it easier to tolerate. People who want to increase their absorption of iron from plant sources can choose to eat vitamin C-containing foods together with iron-containing meals.

Food sources of vitamin C

Vitamin C occurs naturally in a wide variety of vegetables and fruits, such as guava, oranges, kiwi, papaya, strawberries, bell peppers and broccoli. The amount of vitamin C may vary depending on the type of food, its freshness and the cooking method.

Because vitamin C is water-soluble and sensitive to heat, prolonged boiling may cause some of it to be lost. Eating fresh, well-washed vegetables and fruits, or choosing cooking methods that use heat only briefly, such as steaming, helps reduce vitamin C losses and supports adequate nutrient intake.

Scientific evidence

• Vitamin C is an essential nutrient with scientific evidence supporting its role in many aspects of body function, particularly collagen formation, immune system function and the absorption of iron from plant sources.

• Studies confirm that vitamin C is necessary for collagen formation, which is a key component of the skin, blood vessels and connective tissue. It also acts as an antioxidant that helps protect cells from oxidative stress.

• As for the immune system, although vitamin C plays a role in immune cell function, the evidence does not clearly support that taking vitamin C supplements can prevent colds in the general population. Getting enough vitamin C is therefore more important than taking high amounts unnecessarily.

This page is provided for information about nutritional roles only. It is not advice for treating or preventing disease.

Safety information

• The body does not store excess vitamin C, so serious side effects from excessive intake are very rare.

• Intake above 2,000 milligrams per day is not recommended, as it may cause stomach discomfort and diarrhoea.

• High doses may cause kidney stones, although this is uncommon.

• Pregnant women should not take high-dose vitamin C supplements, as this may cause vitamin C deficiency in the baby after birth. Pregnant and breastfeeding women should consult a healthcare professional about the appropriate amount.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
