Overview

Garlic (Allium sativum L.) has a history of use as both a food and an herb in many cultures around the world. What makes garlic unique is not only its pungent smell and hot, spicy taste, but also the chemical reaction that takes place when a garlic clove is crushed, chopped or sliced: the enzyme alliinase reacts with alliin to form allicin, which can then be converted into other sulfur compounds.

Another interesting component of garlic is fructans, carbohydrates that human digestive enzymes cannot break down efficiently. As a result, they can travel through the digestive tract to the large intestine and serve as a food source for certain microorganisms. Microbial fermentation of fructans also leads to the production of short-chain fatty acids, which are associated with the intestinal environment and the function of the cells lining the gut.

From a biochemical perspective, the sulfur compounds in garlic, such as diallyl disulfide (DADS), diallyl trisulfide (DATS) and S-allyl cysteine (SAC), have also been studied for their relationship with antioxidant mechanisms, liver enzyme activity and the body's processes for transforming foreign substances.

How it works in the body

Garlic and how it works in the body

1. Prebiotic properties and gut microorganisms

The human gut is home to a wide variety of microorganisms that play a role in digestion, metabolism and the immune system. The fructans found in garlic can serve as a food source for certain groups of microorganisms, such as Bifidobacterium, although the response to garlic may vary according to the composition of each individual's gut microbiota.

2. Production of short-chain fatty acids and the intestinal environment

When microorganisms in the large intestine use fructans through fermentation, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as acetate, propionate and butyrate are produced. Butyrate in particular is an important energy source for the cells lining the large intestine. This process is part of the relationship between diet, microorganisms and the function of the digestive system.

3. The body's processes for handling foreign substances

The liver plays an important role in transforming various substances through biochemical processes, so that the body can handle and eliminate certain substances. Sulfur compounds in garlic, such as DADS and DATS, have been studied for their relationship with the Nrf2 signalling pathway and the enzyme glutathione S-transferase (GST), which are involved in the body's processes for transforming foreign substances.

4. Oxidative balance and blood vessel health

The sulfur compounds in garlic have also been studied for their relationship with the body's natural antioxidant system, the function of the cells lining the blood vessels and lipid metabolism, another dimension of research into the biological properties of garlic beyond the digestive system.

Unique properties

Forms and consumption of garlic

Garlic can be consumed in many forms, from an everyday cooking ingredient to extracts in dietary supplements. The method of preparation and processing affects its aroma, flavour and the composition of its key compounds.

Fresh garlic: commonly eaten raw or used in cooking; it contains both fructans and the precursors that can be converted into allicin when crushed or sliced.

Cooked garlic: commonly stir-fried, boiled, baked or grilled; heat can affect the activity of the enzyme alliinase and the amount of allicin.

Garlic powder: made from garlic that has been dried and finely ground; commonly used as a seasoning and as an ingredient in dietary supplements.

Garlic oil: distilled types usually contain oil-soluble sulfur compounds such as DADS and DATS, but are not a source of fructans in the way fresh garlic is.

Aged garlic extract: goes through an ageing process that changes its chemical composition, with key compounds such as S-allyl cysteine (SAC).

Black garlic: processed under controlled temperature and humidity; it has a soft texture, a sweet-sour taste and a less pungent smell than fresh garlic.

Scientific evidence

1. Garlic and gut microbiota composition

A 2024 study examined the effects of garlic on gut microorganisms using a model in which microorganisms from human faecal samples were cultured, comparing the responses of microbial communities with different compositions.

The study found that garlic affected the composition and diversity of the microorganisms differently depending on the characteristics of the original microbial community, and also observed an increase in bacteria of the Bifidobacterium group and promotion of the growth of Bifidobacterium adolescentis in culture.

However, this study was a laboratory experiment, not a trial in which people ate garlic directly, so further clinical studies are still needed.

2. Garlic and intestinal transit time

A randomised, double-blind, controlled study from 2023, published in the journal Phytotherapy Research, studied 90 people with metabolic syndrome over 3 months, comparing a group given garlic powder with a group given a placebo.

The study reported results on intestinal transit time and metabolic markers, which provides interesting data on the relationship between garlic and the function of the digestive system.

However, the study was conducted in a specific population, so its results cannot yet be used to confirm that garlic helps improve bowel movements in the general population.

3. Sulfur compounds and enzymes involved in handling foreign substances

A 2019 study examined alliin and sulfur compounds from garlic, assessing their relationship with glutathione levels, the activity of the enzymes glutathione S-transferase (GST) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and the Nrf2 signalling pathway.

Results from animal and cell experiments showed changes in markers related to the antioxidant system and the liver's processes for handling foreign substances, improving understanding of the biochemical mechanisms of sulfur compounds.

However, these findings are not yet evidence confirming effectiveness in detoxifying or cleansing the intestines in humans.

Safety information

Garlic is high in fructans and is classified as a high-FODMAP food, which may cause gas, bloating or abdominal pain in people who are sensitive to this group of carbohydrates, especially those with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS).

Eating raw garlic or large amounts of garlic may cause nausea, stomach discomfort, bad breath and body odour.

Garlic supplements may increase the risk of bleeding. People taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet medicines should consult a doctor before use.

People who are scheduled for surgery should inform their doctor if they are taking dietary supplements containing garlic.

Pregnant and breastfeeding women should consult a doctor before taking garlic in dietary supplement form.

People with a history of garlic allergy should avoid products containing garlic.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
