Overview

Vitamin B6 is one of the B vitamins that the body needs to obtain from food. It serves as a component of coenzymes involved in more than 100 biochemical reactions, especially the metabolism of proteins and amino acids, as well as nervous system function and red blood cell formation.

A key feature of vitamin B6 is its involvement in the synthesis of neurotransmitters such as serotonin, dopamine and GABA, substances involved in communication between nerve cells. At the same time, vitamin B6 also takes part in the metabolism of homocysteine, a substance that has been associated with heart and vascular health in some research.

In addition, vitamin B6 is essential for the normal function of the immune system, being involved in the activity of immune cells and the production of certain substances used in the body's responses.

Key functions

  • Vitamin B6 contributes to normal functioning of the nervous system
  • Vitamin B6 contributes to normal red blood cell formation
  • Vitamin B6 contributes to the normal function of the immune system
  • Vitamin B6 contributes to normal energy-yielding metabolism
  • Vitamin B6 contributes to normal protein and glycogen metabolism

How it works in the body

Vitamin B6 and its mechanisms in the body

1. Homocysteine metabolism and vascular health

Vitamin B6 takes part in converting homocysteine into other substances through amino acid metabolism, working together with enzymes that require vitamin B6 in its active form.

2. Red blood cell formation and energy metabolism

Vitamin B6 is also involved in the synthesis of heme, a component of haemoglobin in red blood cells, making it important for normal red blood cell formation. It also acts as a coenzyme in the metabolism of amino acids and glycogen, so that the body can use nutrients in energy production normally.

3. Neurotransmitter synthesis and brain function

Vitamin B6 in the form of pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (PLP) acts as a coenzyme in the synthesis of several neurotransmitters, such as serotonin, dopamine and GABA, which are involved in communication between nerve cells and the regulation of nervous system function.

4. Immune system function

Vitamin B6 is involved in amino acid metabolism and the production of substances needed for the function of immune cells. Vitamin B6 deficiency may affect some aspects of the immune response.

Unique properties

Food sources of vitamin B6

• Fish, such as salmon and tuna

• Meat and poultry, such as chicken

• Chickpeas and certain legumes

• Potatoes and certain starchy vegetables

• Bananas

• Cereals or foods fortified with vitamin B6

Scientific evidence

• Vitamin B6 has a clear biochemical role in amino acid metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis and nervous system function.

• Studies confirm that vitamin B6 deficiency may affect red blood cell formation and immune system function.

• Vitamin B6 is involved in homocysteine metabolism, but clinical trials do not yet support the use of vitamin B6 to prevent cardiovascular disease in the general population.

• Research on brain function, mood and memory has not yet been able to confirm a benefit of vitamin B6 supplementation in people with adequate nutrient intake.

This page is provided for information about composition and nutritional role only and is not advice for treating or preventing any disease.

Safety information

• Continuous intake of high doses of vitamin B6 may cause peripheral neuropathy, such as numbness or impaired balance.

• Check the total amount of vitamin B6 from all dietary supplements taken, especially when using B-complex vitamins or several supplement products together.

• Vitamin B6 may interact with certain medicines, such as levodopa when not used with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor, and certain anticonvulsants.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
