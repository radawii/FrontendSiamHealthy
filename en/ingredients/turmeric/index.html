Overview

The golden-yellow colour of curries and many other dishes in Thai kitchens comes from a herb we all know well: turmeric. Behind this distinctive colour, however, are natural compounds that have attracted the interest of scientists, especially curcumin, one of the key compounds found in the turmeric rhizome.

Turmeric has the scientific name Curcuma longa L. and belongs to the family Zingiberaceae, the same family as ginger. The part commonly used is the rhizome, an underground stem, not the root as is often thought. The turmeric rhizome contains both natural pigments and essential oils that give it its characteristic aroma and flavour.

The most extensively studied active compounds are the curcuminoids, which consist of curcumin, demethoxycurcumin and bisdemethoxycurcumin. Curcumin has attracted interest in relation to intracellular signalling processes and the response to oxidative stress.

Key functions

  • Contains curcuminoids, which have been studied in relation to the inflammatory response
  • Has been clinically studied in relation to stomach comfort and certain digestive symptoms
  • Has been researched in relation to measures of joint discomfort and mobility

How it works in the body

Turmeric and its mechanisms in the body

1. Relationship to the body's inflammatory processes

Curcumin has been studied in relation to intracellular signalling pathways such as NF-κB, which is involved in regulating signalling molecules in inflammatory processes.

2. Role in the response to oxidative stress

Curcuminoids have a chemical structure associated with antioxidant properties. They have been studied in relation to the function of the cell's defence systems against oxidative stress, including the Nrf2 pathway, which is involved in regulating certain enzymes in the body.

3. Relationship to the digestive system

Turmeric has a history of use in herbal formulas related to the digestive tract, and curcumin has been studied in relation to the response of the gastrointestinal lining and the signalling processes associated with stomach discomfort.

Unique properties

Forms and consumption of turmeric

Turmeric is a herb and spice that has long been bound up with ways of life and cooking. It is consumed in the following forms:

• Fresh turmeric rhizome: the fresh rhizome is pounded, crushed or juiced for use as a main ingredient in curry pastes and southern Thai dishes, or to add natural colour and aroma to food.

• Dried turmeric powder: the turmeric rhizome is sliced, dried and ground into a fine powder for use as a cooking spice, mixed with warm water as a drink, or filled into capsules for consumption.

• Turmeric extract: turmeric undergoes an extraction process to control the curcuminoid content at a consistent concentration. It is commonly filled into dietary supplements in capsule or tablet form.

Scientific evidence

1. Studies on the digestive system

A randomised controlled trial by Kongkam et al. (2023) studied the use of curcumin in people with functional dyspepsia, comparing it with the drug omeprazole and with the two used together.

The study found that participants' dyspepsia symptom scores improved in each group, and no significant difference between groups was found in the main outcomes assessed. However, this study did not have a placebo group, so it cannot be concluded that curcumin is more effective than using no active substance.

In addition, the trial results come from the use of curcumin in a specified amount and form; they are not evidence that general consumption of turmeric can treat indigestion.

2. Studies on joint discomfort

A meta-analysis by Daily et al. (2016) pooled randomised trials of turmeric extract and curcumin in people with joint symptoms and found that some studies reported improved joint discomfort scores and joint function.

However, the studies differed in the type of extract, dose, duration of use and study quality, so larger trials with more consistent standards are still needed.

3. Studies on oxidative stress and inflammatory processes

Curcumin has attracted researchers' interest in relation to biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and substances associated with the body's inflammatory processes.

Some studies have reported changes in these markers after taking curcumin extract, but the trial results are not entirely consistent, and changes in laboratory markers do not necessarily mean there will be clear long-term health benefits.

4. Studies on curcumin absorption

A study by Shoba et al. (1998) examined the use of curcumin together with piperine, the key compound in black pepper, and found that using them together in a specified amount increased the amount of curcumin detected in the body compared with curcumin alone.

However, increased absorption does not confirm increased health effectiveness, and piperine may also affect the metabolism or levels of certain drugs in the body.

This information is provided for education about turmeric. It is not advice for diagnosing, preventing or treating disease.

Safety information

• Concentrated turmeric extract may cause stomach discomfort, nausea, diarrhoea or acid reflux in some people.

• People with liver disease or a history of liver abnormalities should consult a doctor before using concentrated extracts.

• People with gallstones or bile duct abnormalities should consult a doctor before use, as turmeric may affect gallbladder function.

• People taking anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs or other regular medications should consult a doctor or pharmacist before use.

• Pregnant and breastfeeding women should avoid using concentrated extracts in amounts higher than those normally found in food.

• If you develop yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine or unusual fatigue while using the product, stop using it and see a doctor.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
