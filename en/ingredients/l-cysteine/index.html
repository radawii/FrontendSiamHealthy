Overview

L-Cysteine is a sulphur-containing amino acid and is classified as an amino acid that the body can synthesise under normal conditions, through the conversion of the amino acid methionine via various intermediates.

What makes L-cysteine interesting is that it has two distinct important roles: it is a component of the protein keratin, which is involved in the structure of hair and nails, and it is a precursor for producing glutathione, which is part of the cell's system for regulating free-radical balance.

Because of these properties, L-cysteine has attracted considerable interest in biochemistry, protein structure and the study of substances related to the health of the skin, hair and nails.

Key functions

  • A key precursor in the synthesis of glutathione, which plays a role in the cell's antioxidant system
  • Is a component of the protein keratin found in hair, nails and skin
  • Contributes to maintaining protein structure through the formation of disulphide bonds
  • Plays a role in maintaining oxidative balance within cells
  • Takes part in the body's metabolism of amino acids and sulphur compounds

How it works in the body

L-Cysteine and its mechanisms in the body

When the body receives protein from food, the digestive system breaks it down into amino acids and small peptides before they are absorbed and used in various processes, including the production of proteins and compounds necessary for cells. L-Cysteine therefore plays an important role through two main processes, as follows:

1. Glutathione production

Glutathione is a compound made from three amino acids: glutamate, cysteine and glycine. Cysteine is often one of the factors that affects the rate of glutathione synthesis in cells. Glutathione takes part in the system that regulates oxidative balance and in many biochemical processes.

2. Forming and maintaining the structure of keratin

Keratin is a key protein found in hair, nails and skin. The amino acid cysteine can form disulphide bonds between protein chains, which stabilise the keratin structure. These bonds are all involved in the strength and physical characteristics of hair and nails.

Food sources of L-cysteine

• Meat and poultry, such as chicken, pork and beef

• Fish and seafood, such as salmon, tuna and shrimp

• Eggs, such as chicken eggs and duck eggs

• Milk and dairy products, such as milk, yoghurt and cheese

• Beans and legumes, such as soybeans, peanuts and lentils

• Seeds, such as sunflower seeds, pumpkin seeds and sesame

• Grains, such as oats, wheat and whole grains

Scientific evidence

1. Evidence on antioxidant activity

Biochemical studies show that the amount of cysteine available to cells is associated with glutathione synthesis. However, clinical evidence confirming that taking L-cysteine alone can raise glutathione levels or affect long-term health is still limited.

2. Studies on hair and nail health

Research on protein structure supports the role of cysteine in the properties of keratin, but clinical studies on taking L-cysteine to strengthen hair, reduce hair loss or directly care for nails are not yet sufficient to draw clear conclusions about its effectiveness.

3. Evidence on skin and skin pigmentation

Glutathione and substances related to skin pigment formation have been studied, but the results of these studies cannot be used to directly confirm the effectiveness of L-cysteine, because of differences in the substances studied, the way they were used and the outcomes measured.

This page is provided for information about the composition and scientific study of L-cysteine. It is not advice for treating or preventing disease.

Safety information

• People with amino acid metabolism disorders, and pregnant and breastfeeding women, should get advice from a doctor before use, as there may be individual limitations.

• For high-dose intake, do not increase the amount on your own, as data on the safety of continuous high-dose use are still insufficient.

• When using it for skin, hair and nail care, do not expect specific results from taking L-cysteine without supporting clinical evidence.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
