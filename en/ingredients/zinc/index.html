Overview

Zinc is a mineral the body needs only in small amounts, yet it is involved in many biological processes, from the growth and development of the body to cellular function and the balance of various systems.

The body cannot produce zinc on its own, so it must be obtained from food, such as oysters, meat, eggs, dairy products, legumes and certain grains. The absorption of zinc may vary depending on the food source and the composition of the meal.

In addition to zinc from food, zinc is also available in dietary supplement forms such as zinc gluconate, zinc citrate and zinc amino acid chelate, which differ in chemical structure and the amount of zinc they provide. The choice of supplement should therefore take into account need and total zinc intake.

Key functions

  • Zinc contributes to the normal function of the immune system
  • Zinc contributes to normal DNA synthesis
  • Zinc contributes to the maintenance of normal skin, hair and nails
  • Zinc contributes to the maintenance of normal vision
  • Zinc contributes to the protection of cells from oxidative stress
  • Zinc contributes to normal protein synthesis

How it works in the body

What does zinc do?

• Enzyme activity: zinc is involved in the activity of a large number of enzymes in the body

• Protein and genetic material synthesis: plays a role in the synthesis of proteins and DNA

• Cell division and growth: is a key component in the development and repair of tissues

• The immune system: is involved in the development and normal function of immune cells

• Taste and smell: is involved in the normal function of the senses of taste and smell

Zinc and its mechanisms in the body

Once the body absorbs zinc into the bloodstream and transports it to cells, zinc acts as an essential cofactor for the activity of hundreds of enzymes in the body. It binds to the catalytic sites of these enzymes, helping the biochemical processes and nutrient metabolism within cells to proceed normally and continuously.

Beyond its role in enzymes, zinc also has an important structural role at the molecular level, especially as the core of a group of proteins called "zinc fingers", which help stabilise protein structure and work with genetic material to regulate gene expression. As a result, the transmission of genetic information, cell division and cell growth proceed according to their natural cycle.

At the same time, zinc also acts as a cellular signalling agent, helping to maintain the balance of cellular function in various systems of the body.

Unique properties

Food sources of zinc

• Oysters and shellfish, such as crab and shrimp

• Lean meats, such as beef, pork and lamb, and poultry such as chicken breast or turkey

• Egg yolks and dairy products, such as fresh milk, cheese and yoghurt

• Seeds, such as pumpkin seeds, sesame seeds and sunflower seeds

• Nuts and dried beans, such as cashews, almonds, chickpeas and red kidney beans

• Whole grains, such as oats, quinoa, brown rice and wholewheat bread

• Dark chocolate with 70% cocoa or more, and cocoa powder

Scientific evidence

• Nutritional data and research support zinc as an essential mineral for enzyme activity, the immune system, the synthesis of genetic material and the processes of cell growth.

• Forms in supplements: the NIH reports that zinc citrate and zinc gluconate are absorbed to a similar extent, and it is not yet clear which form is better. Comparative data specifically for the amino acid chelate form are still limited.

• The natural role of zinc in the body does not mean that taking zinc supplements will improve everyone's health or provide additional benefits, especially for people who already get enough zinc from food.

This page is provided for information about zinc's nutritional role only and is not advice for treating or preventing any disease.

Safety information

• Zinc is an essential mineral, but excessive intake may cause nausea, stomach pain or disturb the balance of other minerals, especially copper, when high amounts of zinc are taken continuously over a long period.

• Zinc supplements may also interact with certain medicines, such as some groups of antibiotics. People who take regular medication, have underlying health conditions or are considering taking several dietary supplements together should consult a doctor or pharmacist before use.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
