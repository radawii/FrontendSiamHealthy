Green tea is a beverage familiar to many people. It contains key compounds such as catechins and EGCG, which are natural antioxidants. These compounds are currently attracting researchers' interest in relation to energy metabolism, blood vessel function and the protection of cells from damage caused by free radicals.

Overview

Green tea, black tea and oolong tea may differ in colour, aroma and flavour so much that they seem like different kinds of tea, but in fact all three can be produced from the same plant, Camellia sinensis. The key difference lies in how the tea leaves are processed: green tea is heated to inhibit the enzymes that cause the compounds in the leaves to oxidise, which helps preserve more of the catechins compared with fully oxidised teas.

One of the compounds attracting the most interest is EGCG (epigallocatechin gallate), a catechin that belongs to the polyphenol group. Researchers study the association of EGCG with cell function, metabolism and the response to oxidative stress.

Green tea also contains other catechins, such as epicatechin (EC), epicatechin gallate (ECG) and epigallocatechin (EGC), as well as caffeine and the amino acid L-theanine, components that contribute to the distinctive character of tea.

How it works in the body

Green tea and how it works in the body

1. Association with the body's energy use

Catechins, particularly EGCG, and the caffeine in green tea have been studied in relation to the body's energy use, including heat production (thermogenesis) and the use of fat as a main energy source.

2. The role of EGCG in oxidative stress

EGCG has a chemical structure that can interact with free radicals in laboratory experiments, and it has been studied in relation to the systems that regulate the response to oxidative stress within cells.

3. Association with blood vessel function

The polyphenols in green tea have been studied in relation to the function of the cells lining the blood vessel walls, which play a role in regulating the dilation and constriction of blood vessels as well as the response to signalling molecules in the body.

Unique properties

Forms and consumption of green tea

Green tea is a herbal plant and beverage that is popular around the world. It is consumed in many different forms, from an everyday drink to standardised extracts in the health industry, as follows:

• Dried green tea leaves and brewed tea: fresh green tea leaves are heated quickly, by roasting or steaming, to inhibit the enzymes and prevent fermentation, and are then dried. They are brewed with hot water as a drink, giving a natural flavour and aroma.

• Matcha powder: green tea leaves that are shaded before harvest are dried and ground with a stone mill into a fine powder. The whole leaf is drunk without straining, or the powder is used as an ingredient in food and desserts.

• Ready-to-drink green tea beverages: green tea in bottles or cans, available both as natural unsweetened versions and flavoured versions.

• Green tea extract: green tea leaves go through an extraction process to standardise the content of key polyphenols, especially EGCG, as well as L-theanine. It is commonly used in dietary supplements in capsule or tablet form.

Scientific evidence

1. Studies on weight control and metabolism

A systematic review by Jurgens et al. (2012), published in the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, evaluated randomised trials of green tea extract for weight loss and weight maintenance.

The analysis found that green tea use in overweight or obese adults had a relatively small effect on weight loss, and no clearly clinically significant outcomes were found overall.

Although catechins and caffeine have been studied in relation to the body's energy use, the evidence is not yet sufficient to conclude that green tea extract can effectively help with weight loss.

2. Studies on blood vessel function

A Cochrane review by Hartley et al. (2013) evaluated trials of green and black tea on cardiovascular risk factors and found that some studies reported changes in blood lipid levels and blood pressure.

However, most of the trials were relatively short and used different products, so long-term effects on the development of cardiovascular disease cannot yet be confirmed.

3. Studies on oxidative stress

EGCG and catechins have been widely studied for their antioxidant properties, particularly in cell and animal experiments, which have found associations with the processes involved in responding to oxidative stress.

However, results in humans are not entirely consistent, because catechins have limited absorption and are transformed within the body, so it cannot yet be confirmed that taking green tea extract helps prevent cell degeneration or any disease.

Safety information

• The caffeine in green tea may cause insomnia, palpitations, restlessness or stomach discomfort in people who are sensitive to caffeine.

• Concentrated green tea extracts, especially formulas with high amounts of EGCG, may carry a risk of liver injury, although this is uncommon.

• People with liver disease or a history of liver abnormalities should consult a doctor before using green tea extract.

• Pregnant and breastfeeding women should take into account their total caffeine intake from food and beverages, and avoid using concentrated extracts without advice from a healthcare professional.

• Drinking tea close to mealtimes may reduce the absorption of non-heme iron from certain foods, especially in people at risk of iron deficiency.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
