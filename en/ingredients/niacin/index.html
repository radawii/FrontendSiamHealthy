Niacin (Vitamin B3)Connecting Food, Energy and Cellular Function

Niacin, or vitamin B3, plays an important role in the body's energy-yielding processes as a component in the formation of substances called NAD and NADP, which help cells use nutrients from carbohydrates, proteins and fats in various processes. Niacin also contributes to the maintenance of normal mucous membranes of the digestive tract, normal skin and normal nervous system function.

Overview

The foods we eat every day, whether rice, meat or fat, are all important sources of energy for the body. But having nutrients in the body does not mean that cells can use them immediately, because they must still go through many steps of chemical conversion. One of the vitamins involved in these processes is niacin, or vitamin B3.

Niacin is a water-soluble vitamin belonging to the B-complex group and is found in a wide variety of foods, especially meat, fish, poultry, legumes and certain grains. In the body, niacin is the precursor for two important coenzymes: NAD (Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide) and NADP (Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate).

The interest in niacin is not limited to energy-yielding processes; it is also involved in nervous system function and in maintaining normal skin and mucous membranes of the digestive tract, which is why the body needs an adequate intake of this vitamin.

Key functions

  • Contributes to the maintenance of normal mucous membranes of the digestive tract and normal skin
  • Contributes to normal energy-yielding metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins and fats
  • Contributes to normal functioning of the nervous system

How it works in the body

Niacin and its mechanisms in the body

1. Converting nutrients into energy

When we eat, the body breaks down carbohydrates, proteins and fats into substances that cells can use. Niacin is a component in the formation of NAD, which helps accept and donate electrons in the chemical reactions involved in producing energy within cells.

In other words, niacin does not provide energy as food does, but it is one of the nutrients that help the process of using energy from food proceed normally.

2. Maintaining normal skin and mucous membranes of the digestive tract

Skin cells and the cells lining the digestive tract are continuously renewed and repaired. These processes require energy and many steps of biochemical reactions in which NAD and NADP are involved. Adequate niacin intake is therefore part of maintaining the normal function of these cells.

3. Involvement in nervous system function

Nerve cells need energy to maintain cellular balance and to send signals to one another, so niacin is important through the role of NAD in energy production and cellular function. In addition, NAD is involved in the repair of genetic material and in regulating the activity of certain proteins within cells.

Scientific evidence

1. Evidence on energy use from nutrients

Information from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) states that niacin is the precursor of NAD and NADP, coenzymes involved in more than 400 chemical reactions in the body, including the conversion of nutrients into energy.

This role is a recognised nutritional function, but it does not mean that niacin supplementation in people who already get enough vitamin B3 will further increase energy or strength.

2. Evidence on the skin and mucous membranes of the digestive tract

Severe niacin deficiency can cause pellagra, whose main symptoms involve the skin, the digestive system and the nervous system. Adequate niacin intake is therefore important for maintaining the normal function of these tissues.

However, evidence on correcting a vitamin deficiency cannot be used to conclude that taking additional niacin will directly improve the skin of people who are not deficient.

3. Evidence on nervous system function

Niacin plays an important role in the energy production of nerve cells and in the function of NAD, which is involved in various processes within cells.

Although severe niacin deficiency may affect the nervous system, there is not yet sufficient clinical evidence that niacin supplementation in people who already have adequate nutrition will improve memory, concentration or learning ability.

4. Studies on high-dose niacin

The AIM-HIGH and HPS2-THRIVE clinical trials studied high-dose nicotinic acid to evaluate its effects on cardiovascular health in at-risk patients. They found no clear additional benefit on major cardiovascular outcomes when used alongside certain standard treatments, while adverse effects increased.

These studies used niacin at therapeutic drug doses, which differ from the amounts normally obtained from food or ordinary dietary supplements, so their results should not be generalised to niacin in all doses and all forms.

This information is provided to educate about niacin and is not advice for diagnosing, preventing or treating any disease.

Safety information

• High doses of nicotinic acid may cause skin redness, flushing, itching or discomfort.

• High intakes of niacin from dietary supplements or medicines may affect the liver, blood sugar levels and uric acid levels.

• People with liver disease, diabetes or gout, or who take regular medication, should consult a doctor or pharmacist before taking high-dose niacin supplements.

• High-dose niacin should not be used to self-manage blood lipid levels without medical supervision.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
