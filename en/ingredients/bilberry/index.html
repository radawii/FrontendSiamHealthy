Overview

Bilberry may look similar to the blueberry that many people know, but cutting the fruit open reveals an interesting difference: the inner flesh of the bilberry is usually a deep reddish-purple, unlike many cultivated blueberry varieties, which have pale flesh. These colours are not there merely to make the fruit attractive; they come from natural compounds called anthocyanins, which have attracted considerable interest in health research.

Bilberry has the scientific name Vaccinium myrtillus L. and belongs to the family Ericaceae. It grows naturally in forests and temperate zones of Europe and parts of Asia. Although it belongs to the same genus as the blueberry, it is not the same species.

The anthocyanins in bilberry comprise many different compounds. They are a group of natural pigments whose chemical properties are associated with antioxidant activity, so researchers have taken an interest in the role of these compounds in cell and blood vessel function, as well as in matters related to eye health.

Key functions

  • Contains anthocyanins, which contribute to antioxidant activity and eye cell function
  • Has been researched in relation to blood vessel function and blood circulation
  • Contains polyphenols that have been studied in relation to the response to oxidative stress

How it works in the body

Bilberry and its mechanisms in the body

1. The role of anthocyanins in oxidative stress

Anthocyanins are natural pigments in bilberry with antioxidant properties. They can interact with free radicals and with chemical processes associated with oxidative stress.

2. Relationship to eye cell function

Anthocyanins from bilberry have been studied in relation to intracellular signalling processes and the response to cellular stress in the eye.

3. Relationship to blood vessel function

The polyphenols in bilberry have been studied in relation to the function of the blood vessel wall, which helps regulate the dilation and constriction of blood vessels, as well as the response to signalling molecules in the body.

Unique properties

Forms and consumption of bilberry

Bilberry is a fruit of the berry family that is dark blue to purplish-black in both its skin and flesh. Because the fresh fruit is delicate and bruises and spoils easily, it is commonly processed for consumption in the following forms:

• Frozen bilberries and dried fruit: the fresh fruit is quick-frozen or dried for use as an ingredient in cereals and yoghurt, or made into jams and desserts.

• Bilberry juice and herbal tea: the fresh fruit is pressed into ready-to-drink juice, or the dried fruit and leaves are ground for brewing as tea.

• Bilberry extract: bilberries undergo an extraction process to control the anthocyanin content at a consistent concentration. It is commonly filled into dietary supplements in capsule form for convenient intake and a defined amount of the active compounds.

Scientific evidence

1. Studies on eye fatigue

Ozawa et al. (2015) studied people who work with computer screens, using bilberry extract at 480 milligrams per day for 8 weeks. They found that some measures of eye fatigue and symptoms reported by participants improved compared with the placebo group.

However, 80 participants completed the trial, and positive results were not seen for every type of measure, so it cannot yet be concluded that bilberry extract helps reduce eye fatigue in everyone.

2. Studies on the function of the muscles used to adjust focus

A randomised, placebo-controlled trial in 109 adults studied intake of a standardised bilberry extract at 240 milligrams per day for 12 weeks and found significant differences in some measures related to the function of the focusing muscles after using the eyes on screens.

Although the results are interesting, the study examined specific measures; it is not evidence that bilberry can correct short-sightedness or long-sightedness, or improve sharpness of vision in general.

3. Studies on vision in the dark

A systematic review by Canter and Ernst (2004) assessed placebo-controlled trials of anthocyanosides from bilberry and found that most methodologically rigorous trials did not support the claim that bilberry improves night vision in people with normal eyesight.

There is therefore not yet sufficient reliable evidence to claim that bilberry helps people see better at night.

4. Studies on blood vessels and oxidative stress

Anthocyanins and polyphenols from bilberry have been studied in relation to blood vessel function and biomarkers associated with oxidative stress, but human trial results still vary depending on the type of extract, dose and participant group.

It therefore cannot yet be confirmed that taking bilberry helps prevent vascular disease or improves blood circulation to a clinically significant degree.

This information is provided for education about bilberry. It is not advice for diagnosing, preventing or treating disease.

Safety information

• Eating bilberries in amounts normally found in food is generally considered safe.

• People taking regular medications, especially those related to blood clotting, should consult a doctor or pharmacist before using concentrated extracts.

• Pregnant and breastfeeding women should avoid using bilberry extract in amounts higher than those found in food, as safety data are still insufficient.

This information is educational and is not medical advice. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take medicines, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using supplements.
